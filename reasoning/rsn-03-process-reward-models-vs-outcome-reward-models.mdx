A model can arrive at the correct final answer while reasoning incorrectly along the way. Conversely, it can execute almost every intermediate step correctly and still fail because of a single arithmetic error. This observation leads to one of the most important questions in the training and runtime control of reasoning models: **Should an evaluation system judge only the outcome—or every step of the solution process?**

This is where **Outcome Supervision** and **Process Supervision** differ. Outcome Reward Models (ORMs) evaluate a complete solution or its final result. Process Reward Models (PRMs), by contrast, attempt to evaluate individual reasoning steps. In *Let's Verify Step by Step*, Hunter Lightman and colleagues compared both approaches on mathematical tasks and found a substantial advantage for process supervision in their experimental setup: the best process-supervised model solved 78 percent of a representative subset of the MATH test set.[^1]

This finding matters—but it does not mean PRMs “eliminate hallucinations” or reliably detect every mistake. A PRM is itself a learned model. It can be wrong, learn superficial heuristics, and be exploited by strong search procedures. Process supervision is therefore best understood as an **additional verification layer**, not as a mathematical guarantee.

---

## 1. Outcome Supervision: The Result Is What Counts

Outcome supervision is conceptually simple. For an input `x`, a model generates a complete solution `y`. A reward model or deterministic checker then produces a score:

```text
R_outcome(x, y) -> Score
```

For a mathematics problem, that might mean only:

```text
Final answer = 42 -> correct or incorrect?
```

For code, a test suite can act as the outcome verifier:

```text
Patch -> tests run -> 96/96 passed -> high reward
```

This approach scales well when the final result is cheap to verify. It does not require annotation of every intermediate step and can therefore evaluate large volumes of automatically generated solutions.

The problem is information loss. A binary final evaluation does not reveal **where** the solution path went wrong. More seriously, a correct result can have been reached by accident or through logically flawed reasoning.

```text
Step 1: correct
Step 2: incorrect
Step 3: compensating error
Final result: accidentally correct
```

A pure outcome checker sees only the last line.

---

## 2. Process Supervision: Assigning Credit to Intermediate Steps

Process supervision moves evaluation to a finer granularity. A solution is treated as a sequence of steps:

```text
s1 -> s2 -> s3 -> ... -> sn
```

The PRM estimates a quality score for each step or intermediate state:

```text
R_process(x, s1...si) -> Score_i
```

A simplified example:

```text
1. 2 + 2 = 4              -> plausibly correct
2. 4 * 3 = 12             -> plausibly correct
3. 12 / 0 = 0             -> mathematically wrong
4. further steps           -> should not continue unchecked
```

The strength of this approach lies in **credit assignment**. If a search algorithm explores twenty possible paths, a PRM can discard weak branches early instead of waiting until full generation is complete before discovering that the final answer is wrong.

This is especially relevant for test-time compute. A PRM can serve not only as a training signal but also as a **search heuristic** during inference.

---

## 3. What Lightman et al. Actually Showed

OpenAI’s *Let's Verify Step by Step* is a central reference, but it is often interpreted too broadly. Lightman et al. compared process and outcome supervision on the MATH dataset while keeping important parts of the setup constant. Process supervision performed significantly better in their Best-of-N experiments.[^1]

The paper also released **PRM800K**, a dataset containing roughly 800,000 human step-level labels.[^1] The annotations were not free-form essays; they were ratings of individual mathematical solution steps.

The empirical conclusion is therefore relatively narrow:

> For this mathematical reasoning setup, step-level feedback was a more effective signal than evaluating only the final outcome.

The paper did **not** show:

- that PRMs outperform ORMs in every domain,
- that PRMs evaluate without error,
- that a PRM “understands” truth,
- or that process supervision eliminates hallucinations in general.

This limitation matters when the concept is transferred to software engineering, security, or governance.

---

## 4. Why Process Feedback Is Expensive

Outcome labels can be generated automatically for many tasks. A unit test produces `pass/fail`, a compiler returns an exit code, and a solver produces a formal validation result.

Step-level labels are harder. Human annotators must understand whether an intermediate step is correct, relevant, and useful. That creates substantial annotation cost and requires domain expertise.

PRM800K illustrates this effort. Lightman et al. therefore also investigated active-learning strategies to use human labels more efficiently.[^1]

For production systems, this suggests a hierarchy of verification:

```text
Strongest signal:
formal / deterministic checker
        ↓
structured domain validator
        ↓
PRM or specialized LLM judge
        ↓
generic LLM-as-a-Judge
        ↓
generator's own self-evaluation
```

The farther down this hierarchy a system operates, the more important calibration and independent evaluation become.

---

## 5. PRMs as Search Heuristics

Perhaps the most interesting use of a PRM is not in training but in **runtime search**.

Assume a model generates several continuations after each step:

```text
                 -> s2a -> s3a
s1 ->
                 -> s2b -> s3b
                 -> s2c -> s3c
```

A PRM scores the partial paths:

```text
score(s2a) = 0.91
score(s2b) = 0.17
score(s2c) = 0.74
```

The search algorithm can discard `s2b` and concentrate compute on `s2a` and `s2c`. This is where Process Reward Models and inference scaling meet. Snell et al. investigate test-time-compute strategies that use dense process-based verifiers.[^2]

The benefit is efficiency. Instead of completing ten fully wrong solutions, the system can react early.

The drawback is equally important: if the PRM mistakenly assigns a low score to a good path, that path is pruned. **Verifier recall** therefore becomes a critical metric.

---

## 6. Process Supervision in Software Engineering

Transferring the concept to code is attractive, but it is not one-to-one. A reasoning step such as “change the signature of this method” is harder to evaluate automatically than an algebraic equation.

Nevertheless, the principle can be translated into technical gates:

```text
Plan
 -> schema valid?
 -> affected tests identified?
 -> patch compiles?
 -> unit tests green?
 -> security scan clean?
 -> integration test green?
```

Here, no single PRM performs all evaluation. Instead, a **hybrid process verifier** emerges from deterministic and probabilistic components.

Example:

```text
Agent: "I will change the API response."

Verifier 1: OpenAPI schema diff
Verifier 2: TypeScript compiler
Verifier 3: contract tests
Verifier 4: security policy
Verifier 5: reviewer model for architecture context
```

For agentic engineering, this pattern is often more robust than a monolithic LLM judge.

---

## 7. Outcome Supervision Remains Indispensable

The debate is sometimes framed as an either-or choice. That is impractical. Process supervision can detect local errors; outcome supervision checks whether the overall system actually achieved the goal.

A coding agent can execute every planned step correctly and still implement the wrong user story. Conversely, a patch can pass every test even though the intermediate decisions were risky.

A combination is therefore sensible:

```text
Process Gates:
- correct files?
- permitted tools?
- schema respected?
- security rules followed?

Outcome Gates:
- acceptance criteria satisfied?
- tests passed?
- performance SLO met?
- deployment successful?
```

This distinction also creates a governance advantage. Auditors can reconstruct **which controls were applied during execution** and which **outcome controls were applied at the end**.

---

## 8. Reward Hacking: When the Agent Learns the Evaluator

Every reward system creates an optimization target. Once a capable agent receives enough attempts, there is a risk that it learns to exploit a weakness in the evaluator.

With outcome supervision, the classic example is an incomplete test suite. The agent finds an implementation that passes the tests but violates the real specification.

With process supervision, a model can learn to produce **stylistically plausible reasoning steps** that the PRM likes. A judge can therefore be “convinced” without the underlying solution actually improving.

In later work on chain-of-thought monitoring, OpenAI has pointed out that process signals may also have unexpected side effects for reasoning observability; a model optimized too strongly on monitored thought traces can learn to express problematic intentions less visibly.[^3]

For security engineering, this leads to a fundamental principle:

> Never rely on the same model channel simultaneously as generator, sole verifier, and audit authority.

---

## 9. Calibration Matters More Than the Raw Score

A PRM score of `0.92` sounds precise, but it is useful only if we know how that score relates to actual correctness.

A production verifier should therefore be evaluated on internal data for metrics such as:

- **Precision:** How many positively rated steps are actually correct?
- **Recall:** How many correct steps does the verifier recognize?
- **False Reject Rate:** How often does it discard a good search path?
- **Calibration Error:** Does a score of 0.8 correspond roughly to an 80% probability of correctness?
- **Robustness:** How does it behave on adversarial or unusually phrased solutions?

These metrics are more important than a single benchmark score.

---

## 10. From Reward Model to Evidence System

For enterprise agents, “reward model” is often too narrow a term. A better abstraction is an **evidence system** that aggregates multiple sources of evidence:

```text
Evidence
├── deterministic tests
├── static analysis
├── formal schemas
├── runtime telemetry
├── process verifier
├── outcome verifier
└── human approval
```

The agent then does not optimize merely for a scalar reward; it must satisfy a set of policies. Some are hard constraints, others are soft quality objectives.

This is closer to real software engineering, where “works” is not the same as “secure, maintainable, compliant, and performant.”

---

## Conclusion

Process Reward Models address a real problem in modern reasoning systems: a final-only evaluation provides too little information about **where** a solution path went wrong. Lightman et al. demonstrated convincingly on the MATH dataset that, under their setup, process feedback can substantially outperform outcome supervision.[^1]

But a PRM is not an oracle of truth. It is a learned evaluation model and must itself be evaluated, calibrated, and protected against manipulation. In production agent systems, the strongest architecture is therefore usually hybrid: deterministic checkers, process verifiers, outcome gates, and human approvals complement one another.

This architecture also opens the door to a new source of training data. If outcomes can be verified automatically, models can generate and evaluate millions of their own solution attempts. That brings us to **[Part 4: Synthetic Data & Self-Play](/blog/rsn-04-synthetic-data-und-self-play)**.
