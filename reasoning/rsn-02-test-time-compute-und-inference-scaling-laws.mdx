The first major scaling wave of modern language models happened **before deployment**. Larger models were trained with more data and more compute; scaling-law work by Kaplan et al. and Hoffmann et al. made this relationship quantitatively tangible.[^1][^2] Reasoning models move part of that logic into runtime. The central question is no longer only: *How much compute was invested in training?* It is also: **How much compute may this model spend on this specific request?**

Charlie Snell, Jaehoon Lee, Kelvin Xu, and Aviral Kumar systematically investigated in 2024 how additional inference compute can be used. Their result was more nuanced than the popular shorthand “more thinking is always better”: the effectiveness of different test-time-compute strategies depends heavily on task difficulty. With a compute-optimal strategy, they substantially improved efficiency over a simple Best-of-N baseline; in FLOP-matched experiments, a smaller model could even outperform a model 14 times larger on suitable tasks.[^3]

This is not a universal new law of nature, but an **empirical finding under specific models, tasks, and search methods**. Precisely for that reason, it is interesting for platform architecture: inference becomes a controllable resource.

---

## 1. What Test-Time Compute Actually Means

**Test-Time Compute** or **Inference-Time Compute** refers to additional computation spent on a concrete request after model training has been completed. It can take very different forms:

```text
Simple:
Prompt -> one answer

More test-time compute:
Prompt -> multiple candidates -> evaluation -> best answer

Or:
Prompt -> reasoning path -> verifier -> correction -> new path -> answer
```

The simplest variant is **Best-of-N**. A model generates `N` different candidates, and a scorer selects the best one. Formally:

```text
y* = argmax_{y_i in {y1...yN}} V(x, y_i)
```

`V` is a verifier or reward model. The better `V` is at recognizing correctness, the more useful additional sampling becomes. Without a reliable verifier, the system can merely produce many plausible answers and then evaluate them with another probabilistic judge.

Other methods invest compute **within a single reasoning path**: the model generates intermediate steps, checks them, revises decisions, or calls tools. Still other methods combine parallel paths with search algorithms.

The term therefore does not describe one technique, but an **architectural design space**.

---

## 2. Why “Just More Tokens” Is Not Enough

An intuitive strategy is simply to let the model answer for longer. That can work, but it does not have to. If a model starts from the wrong approach, additional tokens may only elaborate the wrong path.

Snell et al. therefore study two fundamentally different classes: search with a process-based verifier and adaptive changes to the answer distribution during inference.[^3] Their most important finding is that the optimal strategy depends on the problem class.

One can think of this as a diminishing-returns curve:

```text
Quality
  ^                   ______
  |              ____/
  |          ___/
  |      ___/
  |  ___/
  +------------------------> Test-Time Compute
          diminishing returns
```

For very easy tasks, additional search is wasteful. For extremely difficult tasks, a weak base model may fail to produce good candidates even with substantial search. The middle range is especially interesting: the model is capable enough to occasionally produce a correct path, but needs additional attempts or verification to find it reliably.

This explains why adaptive allocation can be better than a rigid “100 samples for every prompt.”

---

## 3. The Verifier Is the Real Bottleneck

Test-time compute is often discussed as a property of the generator model. In many methods, however, the **verifier** is at least as important.

An ideal verifier answers questions such as:

```text
Is this intermediate step correct?
Is this final answer correct?
Is this solution better than the alternative?
```

For formal tasks, this role can be deterministic:

- a compiler checks syntax and types,
- a test suite checks observable program behavior,
- a mathematical solver validates constraints,
- Lean or Coq check formal proofs.

For open-ended tasks—such as architecture advice or strategic analysis—there is no perfect checker. Reward models or LLM-as-a-Judge methods are then used, and these have their own error rates and biases.

This is exactly where test-time compute connects with **Process Reward Models**. If a model can evaluate intermediate states rather than only the final answer, search can be stopped or redirected earlier. Part 3 of this series examines that mechanism in detail.

---

## 4. Best-of-N, Majority Voting, and Search Are Not the Same Thing

In practice, very different methods are often grouped under the label “inference scaling.”

### Best-of-N

The model generates multiple candidates; a scorer selects one. The gain comes from **diversity plus selection**.

### Majority Voting / Self-Consistency

Multiple reasoning paths are generated, after which the most frequent final answer is selected. Wang et al. showed in 2022 that self-consistency can improve chain-of-thought reasoning on several tasks.[^4]

### Beam or Tree Search

Intermediate states are explicitly expanded and evaluated. Weak branches are pruned while promising ones are continued.

### Iterative Revision

A first draft is evaluated and then corrected. This resembles an evaluator-optimizer loop more than parallel sampling.

### Tool-Assisted Inference

Additional compute is invested not only in tokens but also in external operations: Python, web search, database access, or code execution.

These methods have different cost profiles. A system that generates ten parallel candidates stresses throughput differently from a single longer reasoning trace. Tool calls can also add network and third-party latency.

---

## 5. Train-Time and Test-Time Compute Are Substitutes—But Only Partially

The fascinating architecture question is: should you train a larger model, or give a smaller model more time to search?

Snell et al. show that additional test-time compute can be more efficient than additional model size in certain regimes.[^3] That does not mean pre-training scaling is obsolete. A smaller model must first have sufficient **proposal quality**. If it never generates a viable solution path, even the best selection strategy cannot help.

A rough cost function might be expressed as:

```text
Total utility = Quality(Model Capability, Search Budget, Verifier Quality)
Total cost    = Amortized Training + Inference Compute + Tool Costs + Latency
```

For a provider handling millions of simple requests, a capable and efficient base model may be cheaper than permanent deep reasoning. For rare, high-value tasks—such as complex code migrations—additional inference can be economically justified.

---

## 6. Adaptive Reasoning Budgets Become a Platform Function

Once reasoning models are used in production, the budget is no longer merely an academic parameter. OpenAI described configurable reasoning levels for o3-mini; later systems such as GPT-5 combine fast and deeper models with routing.[^5][^6]

A production agent gateway can therefore implement rules such as:

```text
if task == "classification":
    route = fast_model
    budget = low
elif task == "code_review":
    route = reasoning_model
    budget = medium
elif task == "security_incident_analysis":
    route = reasoning_model + tools + verifier
    budget = high
```

The decisive quantity is not merely token count. A reasoning budget can include:

- maximum internal tokens,
- maximum number of samples,
- maximum tool calls,
- time limit,
- cost limit,
- search width,
- number of revisions,
- or number of permitted sub-agents.

This makes **compute governance** part of LLMOps.

---

## 7. Reasoning Scaling and Latency SLOs

More test-time compute directly conflicts with conventional service-level targets. A chat interface may expect latency of a few seconds; an autonomous coding agent may be allowed to work for minutes or longer on a complex repository migration.

This leads to different SLO classes:

```text
Interactive Tier     -> low latency, bounded reasoning
Analytical Tier      -> medium latency, more verification
Agentic Batch Tier   -> long runtime, hard cost/tool budgets
```

This is comparable to database systems that distinguish between OLTP and analytical workloads. A single global timeout is often the wrong abstraction for agentic systems.

At the same time, every longer-running execution needs **stopping criteria**. Without limits, an agent can repeatedly explore similar paths and incur cost without adding quality.

---

## 8. A Practical Compute-Optimality Model

For real systems, the question should not be “How much reasoning is maximally possible?” but:

> What is the minimum additional compute budget that produces the required quality gain for this task class?

A practical experiment consists of four steps:

1. **Measure the baseline:** quality, cost, and latency with minimal reasoning.
2. **Increase the budget stepwise:** for example 1x, 2x, 4x, and 8x.
3. **Measure verifier quality separately:** How often does the scorer actually select the correct candidate?
4. **Determine the Pareto frontier:** Which configurations are both worse and more expensive and can therefore be eliminated?

The result is not a universal scaling law, but a **workload-specific inference policy**.

For code, the quality metric could be `pass@1` on an internal test suite. For RAG, a combination of retrieval recall and factual correctness. For security reviews, precision and recall of detected vulnerabilities. Only when such metrics exist can additional compute be justified economically.

---

## 9. The Dark Side: Reward Hacking and Search over Flaws

More samples do not only increase the chance of finding a good solution. They also increase the chance of exploiting a **weakness in the verifier**. This problem is known from reinforcement learning as reward hacking.

If an automated test covers only part of the specification, a coding agent can find a patch that passes the tests while making the real behavior worse. With an LLM judge, intensive search can discover text that convinces the judge even though it is objectively weak.

This leads to a security principle:

> The more search budget an optimizer receives, the more robust the evaluation system must be against Goodhart effects and manipulation.

Test-time compute should therefore be combined with **diverse verification channels**: unit tests plus static analysis, formal schemas plus integration tests, LLM judge plus deterministic fact checking.

---

## 10. What “Inference Scaling Law” Does Not Mean

The term can suggest a simple power law analogous to pre-training scaling. The research landscape is more complicated. Snell et al. specifically show that optimal allocation is **prompt- and difficulty-dependent**.[^3]

Three limitations are crucial:

- Results from mathematics benchmarks do not automatically transfer to open-ended enterprise tasks.
- Additional compute can reinforce wrong solutions when verifiers are weak.
- Improvements must be weighed against latency, cost, and energy consumption.

Inference scaling is therefore less a single law than a new **optimization surface**.

---

## Conclusion

Test-time compute changes the economics of AI. Compute is no longer invested exclusively in advance and “baked into” a fixed model; it can be allocated dynamically to a task at runtime. Research such as Snell et al. shows that, under suitable conditions, this strategy can be surprisingly efficient and allow a smaller model with additional search to outperform a much larger one.[^3]

The crucial addition, however, is this: **compute alone is not enough.** Candidate quality, the search strategy, and especially the verifier determine whether additional computation helps.

This leads directly to the next topic. If a system generates multiple reasoning paths, it needs an answer to the question: *How can it evaluate not only the outcome but the quality of each individual step?*

That is the subject of **[Part 3: Process Reward Models vs. Outcome Reward Models](/blog/rsn-03-process-reward-models-vs-outcome-reward-models)**.

## Notes on the Sources

The widely repeated statement that a smaller model can beat a model “10 times larger” is too broad. Snell et al. report a FLOP-matched comparison with a **14-times-larger model for specific problems** where the smaller model already has a non-trivial success rate. That condition is essential to the interpretation.[^3]
