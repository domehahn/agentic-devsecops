# How Knowledge Emerges in Large Language Models

A large language model does not begin training with concepts, facts, syntax, or even a notion of language. It begins as a parameterized numerical function whose weights are initialized so that its outputs are essentially uninformative. The remarkable part of modern language modeling is therefore not that a trained model can answer *Paris is the capital of France*. The deeper question is how millions or billions of gradient updates transform an initially meaningless function into a system whose internal activations support such a response.

The most useful answer is neither “the model stores facts like a database” nor “the model merely predicts words.” Both descriptions are incomplete.

An LLM is trained by next-token prediction, but the cheapest way for a sufficiently expressive network to become good at that task is to internalize regularities about language, entities, relationships, style, code, causality-like correlations, discourse, and many other structures present in its training distribution. What we call *knowledge* is therefore better understood as a collection of **distributed statistical representations and computational circuits that help minimize predictive loss**.

This distinction matters because it explains both the impressive capabilities and the epistemic weaknesses of LLMs.

## Before Meaning: A Function with Random Parameters

Let a model with parameters \(\theta\) estimate a conditional distribution over the next token:

\[
p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})
\]

At initialization, \(\theta\) contains no meaningful encoding of Paris, France, gravity, Python, or Shakespeare. The function exists mathematically, but its mapping from context to output probabilities is nearly arbitrary.

Training gives the function structure.

Given a sequence:

```text
Paris is the capital of France
```

a causal language model sees prefixes and learns to increase the probability of the observed continuation. For the prefix:

```text
Paris is the capital of
```

the training target may be:

```text
France
```

The model produces a probability distribution over its vocabulary. Cross-entropy loss for the observed token \(y\) is:

\[
\mathcal{L} = - \log p_\theta(y \mid x_{<t})
\]

Nothing in this objective explicitly says:

> Store the fact that Paris is the capital of France.

The optimization target says:

> Adjust the parameters so that sequences like the training data become more probable.

That apparently modest objective is the engine behind representation learning at scale.

## Error Correction Does Not Update a Fact Slot

After the forward pass computes the loss, backpropagation applies the chain rule through the computational graph.

For a parameter \(\theta_i\):

\[
\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_i}
\]

measures how a small change in that parameter would affect the loss for the current example.

An optimizer then updates parameters, conceptually:

\[
\theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - \eta \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_i}
\]

where \(\eta\) is the learning rate.

The important architectural implication is that **the update is distributed**. The error signal propagates through the output projection, residual pathways, feed-forward blocks, attention transformations, normalization layers, embeddings, and other learned parameters.

There is no required single variable:

```text
capital_of_france = "Paris"
```

Instead, the example slightly reshapes a high-dimensional system whose parameters are shared across enormous numbers of contexts.

## Why Predicting Tokens Forces Representation Learning

To reduce loss across diverse corpora, the model benefits from encoding abstractions that make many predictions easier simultaneously.

Examples include:

- grammatical roles;
- semantic similarity;
- entity identity;
- discourse structure;
- programming-language syntax;
- common algorithms;
- geographic relationships;
- stylistic conventions.

These abstractions are not inserted manually. They arise because they are useful compression structures for prediction.

A simplified picture:

```text
surface tokens
     ↓
embeddings
     ↓
contextual transformations
     ↓
latent features
     ↓
next-token distribution
```

The latent features need not correspond one-to-one with human concepts. A single neuron is rarely “the France neuron,” and a single fact need not live in one parameter.

## Distributed Knowledge Does Not Mean Completely Unstructured Knowledge

Saying that knowledge is distributed should not be interpreted as saying that nothing is localizable.

Mechanistic-interpretability and model-editing research has shown that particular layers, activations, attention patterns, and feed-forward pathways can be disproportionately important for certain behaviors.

Geva et al. argued that Transformer feed-forward layers can behave in ways resembling key-value memories.[^1] ROME and subsequent model-editing work demonstrated that factual associations can sometimes be modified by targeted parameter interventions.[^2]

These findings do not turn the model into a relational database. A more accurate hierarchy is:

```text
fact behavior
   depends on
distributed circuits
   with
partially localizable contributions
```

## Parametric Memory Versus Exact Memorization

LLMs both generalize and memorize.

### Generalization

The model learns a pattern that transfers beyond exact training examples.

### Memorization

The model can also retain unusually specific sequences, especially when they are duplicated, rare but repeatedly observed, highly distinctive, or overrepresented.

Carlini et al. demonstrated extraction of memorized training sequences from language models, highlighting privacy implications.[^3]

Thus:

```text
statistical knowledge
≠
memorized training record
```

Both can coexist.

## Factuality Is Not the Optimization Objective

Pretraining does not contain a built-in truth checker.

Cross-entropy rewards compatibility with the observed training distribution.

If training data contains contradictions, fiction, outdated statements, propaganda, satire, or errors, the model is still optimized to model those patterns.

The objective:

\[
\min_\theta \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D}[-\log p_\theta(x)]
\]

does not ask whether \(x\) is true.

It asks whether the model assigns probability appropriately under dataset \(D\).

This is the core reason factuality is an emergent and incomplete property rather than a guaranteed invariant.

## Sequence Probability Connects Language, Facts, and Reasoning-Like Behavior

Autoregressive modeling factorizes the joint probability:

\[
p(x_1,\dots,x_T)
=
\prod_{t=1}^{T} p(x_t \mid x_{<t})
\]

If a model predicts each token using the preceding context, improving next-token prediction may require learning which entity a pronoun refers to, which variable was defined earlier, what premise constrains a conclusion, or which API signature is valid.

These abstractions are useful because they reduce sequence prediction error.

## Backpropagation Through Attention

For Transformer attention:

\[
Q = XW_Q,\quad K = XW_K,\quad V = XW_V
\]

\[
\mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)
=
\mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\]

If the model underpredicts the correct token, gradients flow into \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\) and upstream representations.

Repeated across training, this changes which contextual tokens are compared, how strongly positions influence one another, what features are extracted, and how information is transported.

Attention does not store “meaning” directly. It is one mechanism through which the network learns to route and combine information.

## Scale Changes the Internal Representation

Scaling-law research found empirical power-law relationships between model performance, model size, data, and compute over significant regimes.[^4] Chinchilla later showed that many large models were undertrained relative to available compute and that model size and training tokens should be scaled together more carefully.[^5]

More parameters do not simply mean “more database slots.”

They provide greater representational capacity and richer conditional computation, but scaling does not turn the objective into truth verification.

## Why Knowledge Can Be Inconsistent

A language model conditions on context.

Therefore question wording, system instructions, examples, language, sampling parameters, and retrieved context can change the generated answer.

The model estimates:

\[
p(y \mid context)
\]

Change the context and the distribution changes.

Parametric knowledge therefore has a **contextual retrieval problem**.

## Knowledge at Inference Time Is Reconstructed

When a model answers a question, the process is not:

```text
query → database lookup → fact
```

It is:

```text
query tokens
   ↓
contextual hidden states
   ↓
layer-by-layer computation
   ↓
next-token probability distribution
   ↓
generated token
   ↓
repeat
```

A useful conceptual phrase is:

> **Parametric knowledge is executable statistical structure.**

## Hallucination Is a Structural Consequence

When evidence is weak, the decoder still produces a probability distribution.

Unless training and alignment strongly encourage abstention, generation continues.

Retrieval, tools, citations, abstention policies, and external verification are therefore **system-level epistemic controls** rather than cosmetic add-ons.

## Training, Post-Training, and the Difference Between Knowledge and Behavior

Pretraining shapes broad representations. Post-training changes how the model uses them.

Instruction tuning and preference optimization can alter response style, refusal behavior, tool-use tendencies, and prioritization of helpfulness or safety.

InstructGPT showed the effectiveness of human-feedback-based post-training.[^6] DPO later simplified part of preference optimization.[^7]

But alignment does not create a truth oracle.

## System-Level Consequences

### Freshness
Weights do not automatically update when the world changes. Use retrieval or tools for volatile facts.

### Auditability
A generated claim does not inherently carry provenance. Attach source evidence where provenance matters.

### Privacy
Memorized training examples can create leakage risk.

### Correction
Changing one factual behavior through model editing or fine-tuning is harder to bound than updating a database row.

### Reliability
Correct parametric knowledge does not guarantee correct generation.

## Conclusion

What we call *knowledge* in an LLM is not a library of explicit propositions.

It emerges from repeated optimization of a next-token prediction objective over massive datasets. Cross-entropy provides the error signal; backpropagation distributes it through the network; optimization reshapes embeddings, attention pathways, feed-forward circuits, and output projections.

The central engineering lesson is:

> **LLMs represent useful regularities in a form optimized for probabilistic generation rather than explicit truth-preserving storage.**

Use the model where flexible inference is valuable. Use databases, retrieval, tools, policy engines, and verifiers where exactness, freshness, provenance, or formal correctness are required.
