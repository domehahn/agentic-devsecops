MLOps solves an important production problem: how to build, test, deploy, retrain, and monitor machine-learning systems.

AI Engineering asks a broader question:

> How do we engineer AI-enabled systems that remain useful, trustworthy, robust, secure, maintainable, and aligned with human needs in real operational environments?

That expansion is significant because an AI component is only one part of an AI system.

## AI Engineering was being formalized before the generative-AI boom

In September 2019, Carnegie Mellon's Software Engineering Institute published **"AI Engineering: 11 Foundational Practices,"** aimed at organizations building, acquiring, and integrating AI capabilities into business and mission systems.[^sei-2019]

In 2021, SEI described AI Engineering as an emerging field combining principles from systems engineering, software engineering, computer science, and human-centered design.[^sei-2021]

This history matters.

AI Engineering did not begin with chatbots in 2022.

The engineering problem already existed: turning AI capability into reliable systems.

## The system boundary expands again

Software engineering can model a system as:

```text
software
+
infrastructure
+
users
```

AI engineering may need:

```text
software
+
infrastructure
+
data
+
models
+
evaluation
+
human interaction
+
operational environment
```

The behavior of the total system depends on all of them.

<Architecture>
AI Engineering is not "software engineering plus a model API." The AI component changes how behavior is specified, tested, monitored, and trusted.
</Architecture>

## Requirements become harder to express

Traditional software often has deterministic expectations:

```text
input X → output Y
```

AI systems may have statistical or qualitative requirements:

```text
accuracy ≥ threshold
unsafe response rate ≤ threshold
latency ≤ threshold
performance across subgroups
robustness under distribution shift
```

This makes evaluation a central engineering concern.

## Risk expands beyond vulnerabilities

Security remains necessary.

But trustworthy AI also raises questions such as:

- reliability,
- validity,
- explainability,
- privacy,
- harmful failure modes,
- bias,
- misuse,
- human oversight.

NIST's AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, published in January 2023, is explicitly aimed at organizations designing, developing, deploying, or using AI systems and provides a lifecycle-oriented framework for managing AI risk.[^ai-rmf]

<KeyTakeaway>
DevSecOps asks whether software can be delivered and operated securely. AI Engineering must additionally ask whether AI behavior remains acceptable, measurable, and controllable.
</KeyTakeaway>

## Existing disciplines still matter

AI Engineering inherits rather than discards:

```text
software architecture
CI/CD
DevOps
DevSecOps
supply-chain security
observability
systems engineering
```

Then adds concerns around:

```text
data
models
evaluation
uncertainty
human interaction
AI-specific risk
```

This is the recurring pattern in the history of engineering disciplines.

A new layer appears because the system contains new failure modes.

## From artifacts to behavior

The most important conceptual change may be this:

Software engineering traditionally puts strong emphasis on the artifact:

```text
code + configuration
```

AI engineering must put stronger emphasis on observed behavior:

```text
system + context + input → outcome
```

That is why evaluation, monitoring, red teaming, and governance become first-class engineering activities.

The next major transition accelerates this trend: generative AI and agentic systems make outputs open-ended and can connect models directly to tools and external systems.
